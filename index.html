01 / Research question

Family political socialization and voting

Can family political socialization shape electoral participation among university students in Kazakhstani parliamentary elections?
Population · university students aged 18–24Context · KazakhstanOutcome · voting in parliamentary elections

Damira Salimova

02 / Epistemological positioning

The driving paradigm

My research belongs to the positivist school of thought.

This research assumes that:

  • Reality is solid and “out there.”
  • Abstract concepts can be measured from the outside.

How it fits my question

  • My question is empirical: I can ask students about political discussions at home and whether they voted.
  • It is testable: I can compare discussion frequency with voting.
  • It is scoped to university students aged 18–24 and Kazakhstani parliamentary elections.

03 / Relevance

Why I think it’s worth asking

Voting under a hybrid regime

In Kazakhstan, voting is not simply a sign of democracy. It can show whether citizens believe that the benefits of casting a ballot are greater than its costs under existing political conditions.

The influence of family

Family is one of the most influential social institutions in a person’s life and an important agent of political socialization.

Future legitimacy

As older cohorts are gradually replaced by younger generations, understanding why young people vote—or do not vote—has implications for the future perceived legitimacy of the regime.

04 / Literature review

Three camps in the literature

Camp 1

Social Learning Theory

Political behavior is learned through observation, communication, and modeling.

Camp 2

Status Transmission Theory

Political participation reflects inherited socioeconomic resources and inequalities.

Camp 3

Kazakhstani context

Research examines youth political culture and participation within Kazakhstan’s political conditions.

05 / Literature review

Camp 1 · Social learning and family transmission

Core idea

Children can learn political behaviors, values, and attitudes by observing agents of political socialization, especially parents.

Bandura (1977) and Jennings et al. (2009) — seminal works and my core theory

What the evidence suggests

  • Civic talk with family → youth political knowledge and civic practices (Ekström & Östman, 2013).
  • Verbal encouragement → the likelihood of young adult voter turnout increases by 30% (Siegel-Stechler, 2021).
  • Parents’ voting and family discussions → higher turnout intention among adolescents (Kestilä-Kekkonen et al., 2025).
  • Parents’ voting → higher adult-child turnout beyond parental education (Gidengil et al., 2016).

06 / Literature review

Camp 2 · Status transmission

Competing explanation

Intergenerational political reproduction is driven by inherited socioeconomic status (SES), parental education, and structural resources rather than household communication or behavioral modeling. The explanation is based on inequalities.

Verba & Schlozman (2005) · Schlozman, Verba & Brady (2013)

What the evidence shows

  • Inheritance of parental SES can matter more than political learning (Verba & Schlozman, 2005).
  • Family and community backgrounds substantially shape electoral participation (Lahtinen et al., 2019).
  • Parental education matters, while parents’ voting also has a role beyond education (Gidengil et al., 2016).
Why it matters for my proposal: Family resources may influence both discussions at home and students’ voting. This is the potential and most significant confounding factor I need to control in this research.

07 / Kazakhstan context and contribution

Camp 3 · Kazakhstan’s political context

What has been studied

  • Fewer young people rely on traditional sources of political information, including family.
  • Young people distrust the judiciary and police—an important issue when studying parliamentary elections.
  • Young people highly value civic responsibility.

Baizhumakyzy et al. (2025) · Tolen et al. (2026)

What is my contribution?

  • Test Western socialization models in a hybrid regime.
  • Attempt to explain the role of family political discussions in youth electoral turnout.
Value of the research: A few studies examine youth political participation in Kazakhstan. In the sources I have reviewed, I did not find research directly testing the relationship between family political discussions and youth electoral turnout. I want to test Social Learning Theory specifically in the context of Kazakhstan.

This is the gap I see in the sources I have read so far.

08 / Theory and hypotheses

Social learning theory and my hypothesis

My reasoning is deductive: I start from social learning theory. Frequent conversations at home may help students understand political events and develop a sense of civic duty. I then ask whether those students are more likely to vote.

IVFrequent and consistent political discussions at home
→
MechanismTransmission and development of youth’s political knowledge and sense of civic duty
→
DVVoting in parliamentary elections
H1: University students who discuss politics at home more frequently and consistently are more likely to vote in Kazakhstani parliamentary elections.

This is my proposed mechanism, not a finding. Political knowledge may not turn into voting if students see little value in elections.

09 / Variables and mechanism

How the proposed relationship works

IV

Political discussions at home

This choice follows Social Learning Theory: observational and verbal modeling depends on the “frequency, duration, rate, and saliency of modeling cues.”

Bandura (1977)

Mechanism

Political learning

Frequent discussions may develop young people’s political knowledge, democratic values, and civic practices. These may strengthen their sense of civic responsibility, which may then encourage electoral participation.

Jennings et al. (2009) · Ekström & Östman (2013) · Baizhumakyzy et al. (2025)

DV

Electoral participation

Whether a student votes in Kazakhstani parliamentary elections.

Important: This is a proposed mechanism. Different studies support its separate stages, but no single study establishes the complete causal chain in Kazakhstan.
